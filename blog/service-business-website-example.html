Service Business Website Example: A Clearer Path to an Enquiry

Fictional demonstration: This is an original teaching example created by ReachX. The business, page copy and enquiry journey below are fictional. They do not represent a client project, a live website or measured traffic, rankings or sales results.

A visitor considering a service usually needs three answers: does this business handle my problem, what would I receive, and how do I start? A website can look polished while leaving those questions unanswered.

This example shows how an independent operations consultant could present one service more clearly. The fictional consultant helps small service teams document everyday work: handing a new customer to delivery, following up an enquiry, and assigning responsibility for recurring tasks. We will connect that offer to a page title, useful internal links and an enquiry form.

Give the first screen a specific job

Here is the fictional starting version:

Unlock your business potential

We deliver innovative solutions that transform your organisation and take performance to the next level.

Button: Learn more

The copy does not identify the service, the audience or the next step. A business owner looking for help with a messy customer handover must guess whether the consultant is relevant.

Here is a proposed replacement for this demonstration:

Operations consulting for small service teams

Make customer handovers, enquiry follow-ups and recurring tasks easier to manage. Start with a review of one workflow and a written plan explaining what to change, who owns each step and what to check next.

Primary button: Discuss your workflow

Supporting link: See what a workflow review includes

The new heading names the service and audience. The paragraph describes the work and deliverable. Each link has a different purpose: visitors who are ready can enquire; visitors who need details can read the scope.

This is a copy decision to test, rather than evidence of a conversion increase. A real consultant should replace the sample with their actual expertise, scope and delivery process.

Explain what the customer receives

“Tailored consulting solutions” leaves the buyer with more questions. A useful service section should make the proposal easier to understand and compare.

For this fictional workflow review, the page could say:

What the workflow review includes

We discuss one recurring process with the person responsible for it, map the current steps, and identify unclear handovers or missing follow-ups. You receive a written workflow map and a prioritised action list with suggested owners for the next steps.

Bring to the discussion: A description of the process, the tools involved and one example of where work gets delayed. Do not send customer records or confidential documents through the enquiry form.

Scope: The review covers one agreed workflow. Software configuration, team training and implementation require a separate scope.

These details let a visitor assess fit before submitting an enquiry. They also give the eventual conversation a clear starting point. A real service page should state any prerequisites, exclusions and commercial terms accurately; sample scope is not a ReachX service commitment.

Connect the title to the page's subject

The fictional page title begins as “Home | Consulting Solutions”. A more descriptive candidate is:

Operations Consulting for Small Service Teams | Example Consultant

The proposed title reflects the service explained on the page. “Example Consultant” is a placeholder for the fictional business name. It should be replaced with a real business name before anyone adapts the example.

Google recommends concise, descriptive titles and can generate a search-result title using several page signals. A title change does not guarantee that Google will display those exact words. See Google's guidance on title links.

“Operations consulting for small service teams” is an illustrative topic here. We have not validated its search volume or competitiveness. A real business would research the language its customers use and compare it with relevant Search Console queries before choosing its final wording.

Use links that answer the next question

A useful page structure for the fictional consultant could be:

Visitor questionPage or sectionLink wording
Is this the service I need?Workflow review service pageExplore the workflow review
What does the deliverable look like?A clearly labelled demonstrationSee a sample workflow map
How do I prepare?Preparation guidePrepare for a workflow review
How can I discuss my situation?Enquiry sectionDiscuss your workflow

Each destination needs to contain the promised information. A sample workflow map should be marked as a demonstration, and a preparation guide should offer practical instructions rather than repeat the sales page.

Use ordinary links with readable labels and meaningful destinations. Google explains that crawlable links help it find pages, while relevant anchor text helps people and Google understand the linked page. See Google's link guidance.

For an existing site, map this journey to useful pages you already have. Before changing page addresses, review current links and plan redirects where needed.

Make the enquiry match the offer

The button promises a discussion about a workflow. The form should continue that conversation. For this demonstration, a concise starting form asks for:

  • Name and email address.
  • Business website, if there is one.
  • The workflow the visitor wants to discuss.

A helpful prompt for the final field would be: “For example, what happens between a new customer signing up and your team beginning the work?” Avoid demanding sensitive customer information at this stage.

The confirmation should explain that the enquiry has been received and what happens next. Only state a response time if the real business can meet it. Test that the enquiry reaches the intended inbox and that the recipient can reply.

Check the complete journey on a phone and with a keyboard: the service link, form labels, error messages, submission and confirmation. Attractive styling is useful when the visitor can still finish the task.

Measure what the redesign actually changes

This demonstration shows a proposed information structure and copy. It cannot establish that a real site will receive more impressions, clicks or qualified enquiries.

For a live project, keep a dated record of the pages changed and measure these separately:

  • Search discovery: Is the intended page indexed? Which relevant queries and countries produce impressions?
  • Search visits: Which queries lead to clicks on that service page? Separate searches for the business name from other searches where the available data allows it.
  • Enquiries: How many genuine enquiries arrive, and which ones match the service, scope and audience?
  • Customer understanding: Do enquiry conversations reveal repeated questions that the page should answer?

Use comparable reporting periods, exclude your own test enquiries, and note other changes such as advertising, seasonal demand or a new offer. With few visits, results can fluctuate enough that a percentage change says little. Track the actual counts and give the page time to collect useful evidence.

Apply the example to your own service

Write one sentence identifying the customer and problem. Describe the deliverable, explain the limits of the service, and choose a next step that your team can fulfil. Then check that the page title, links and enquiry journey all describe the same offer.
